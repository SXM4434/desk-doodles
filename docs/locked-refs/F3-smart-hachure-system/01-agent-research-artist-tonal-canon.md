# Tonal-to-Mark Conversion: The Artist Canon

Research brief on how artists translate gray-value into hatching / cross-hatching / stippling / pen-and-ink marks. Every claim below is sourced to a passage actually read.

## 1. Canonical references

**Erika Lancaster's two guides** (erikalancaster.com) lay out seven shading techniques (parallel hatching, cross-hatching, contour, stippling, tick, woven, scribbling) and the universal density principle: lighter areas use fewer lines, darker areas use multiple overlapping layers.

**Virtual Instructor (Matt Fussell)** sets the four-technique canon (hatching, cross-hatching, random/scribble, stippling) and the controlling variables: spacing and crossing frequency.

**Sketchbooks.org** gives a five-step build sequence: light directional hatch → layered hatches with directional shift → tighter spacing/denser intersections → preserve blanks for highlights → break uniformity.

**Craftsy (6 Forms of Hatching)** maps each form to a tonal register: parallel = full range via density; contour = mid-tones with dimensional emphasis; cross-hatch = mid-to-dark; fine cross-hatch = full range with subtle transitions; basket/woven = mid-to-dark; tick = mid-to-dark by accumulation.

**Art is Fun (Thaneeya McArdle)** states the foundational constraint explicitly: pen-and-ink "cannot use pressure variation" the way pencil does — value is built through density and pattern, and techniques are routinely combined within one drawing.

**John Muir Laws** (johnmuirlaws.com) adds the perceptual rule: "bold and widely spaced lines" read as lines; "lighter and closer together" lines disappear into tone. He warns explicitly about right-angle and acute-angle crosses (screen-door artifacts).

**HelloArtsy's 4-step crosshatch scale** is the closest thing to a numerical reference: 1 set / 2 sets / 3 sets / 4 sets of lines at ~1/8" spacing, each new step adding one directional layer — value rises through layer count, not gap.

**Wikipedia's Hatching entry** confirms the master-canon variable list: "length, angle, closeness, and other qualities of the lines… By increasing quantity, thickness and closeness, a darker area will result."

**Dürer / Old Masters** (thelax.art, thecollector.com, Wikipedia): Dürer perfected "double hatching" — layering a second pass of finer lines over the first — and used different burin tips for line-weight variation within a single tonal field. His tonal ladder is layer-count + line-weight, not just spacing.

**Loomis (Successful Drawing)** in the search snippet on jacksonsart.com: "vary lines by using thick and thin lines"; build shading in layers from light to dark. Loomis' axis bias is weight + layers (consistent with his pencil-trained illustrator lineage).

**Claudia Nice** (Artists Network): density + direction give "the illusion of light, shadow, and form"; softer textures use "more widely spaced and subtle hatching," rough textures use closer and angular lines. Material-coupling is part of her mark-selection.

**Modern editorial / minimalist illustrators**: Christoph Niemann (itsnicethat.com) lives at "make imperfections fun" — line only, almost no tonal fill; tone via figure-ground, not hatching. Saul Steinberg (nga.gov / bookforum.com) is line-as-thought, with virtually no tonal hatching — proof that "leave it white" is itself a legitimate end of the tonal scale. Wesley Allsbrook (commarts.com / wedesignstudios.com) uses loose brush ink-lines and saturated color, not hatched tone — relevant because she shows when an artist refuses hatching in favor of weight + speed. Nigel Peake (grokipedia.com / mapmagazine.co.uk) uses "precise, repetitive lines" plus "occasional dots and curves" — a hatch+stipple combo for textural rather than tonal effect.

## 2. The multi-axis vocabulary

Five axes, each independently attested, often combined:

- **Gap / density** — primary axis across nearly every source. Lancaster, Virtual Instructor, Art is Fun, Wikipedia, HelloArtsy. Closer = darker.
- **Layer count / direction sets** — HelloArtsy's 4-step scale, Dürer's "double hatching," Lancaster's "second or even third layer." Each added directional pass adds one notch of tonal weight.
- **Line weight / thickness** — Wikipedia ("quantity, thickness and closeness"), Smashing Pencils ("thick in darker regions… thinner toward the lighter"), Loomis. This is the dominant axis when a single pass must imply both shadow and light.
- **Pressure / taper** — Smashing Pencils ("greater pressure and slower speeds will create thicker lines"); Sketchbooks.org folds "rhythm, pressure, and intersection angles" together. Maps cleanly onto perfect-freehand's `thinning`/`size`.
- **Color / opacity** — Laws makes the perceptual case explicitly: "lighter and closer together" lines stop reading as line and start reading as tone. Stippling sources (Number Analytics, Skillshare blog) name dot-thickness as a secondary value lever. Direct opacity manipulation is mostly a digital extension of these.

**Tonal-stair bias by author**: Houghton-style "5-value scale" results from the search are not Houghton's; the closest canonical 4-value stair is HelloArtsy's (layers-dominant). Lancaster/Virtual Instructor stairs are gap-dominant. Loomis/Dürer stairs are weight + layers. **There is no single canonical stair** — the bias depends on tool (engraving burin = layers; flexible nib = pressure; tech pen = density).

## 3. Mark-type selection by tonal context

- **Hatching** — works light to dark; default for smooth/man-made surfaces (Lancaster, Virtual Instructor).
- **Contour hatching** — mid-tones; selected when form-description matters more than max darkness (Craftsy, Virtual Instructor).
- **Cross-hatching** — mid-to-dark; "darker values faster" (Lancaster), "deeper shadows than hatching alone" (Dürer sources).
- **Fine cross-hatching** — full range with subtle transitions; chosen when blending matters (Craftsy).
- **Stippling** — full range but slow; selected for "highly realistic results" (Virtual Instructor) and soft / botanical surfaces (Nice).
- **Scribbling / scumbling** — foliage, hair, organic textures (Lancaster, Art is Fun); also Laws' "tiny overlapping circles" as a tonal under-layer.
- **Tick hatching** — mid-to-dark accumulation, "best with a thicker pen" (Craftsy); a stipple/hatch hybrid.

Selection is driven jointly by **target darkness** and **surface material** — Nice ties this explicitly.

## 4. Edge handling

- **Line-weight transition at the boundary**: thicker on shadow side, thinner on lit side (Jonathan Case via search; Smashing Pencils). Outline is not constant — it tapers.
- **Pen-size strategy**: thicker pens (e.g., 0.5mm) for outlines and fully-shaded areas; thinner pens (e.g., 0.1mm) for hatch lines and details (Smashing Pencils).
- **Avoid orthogonal crosses at edges**: Laws warns right-angle cross-hatch reads as "screen door"; offset the second pass 15–30° off perpendicular.
- **Direction shift across boundaries**: Sketchbooks.org's "slight directional shifts to deepen tones" — the second layer is rarely exactly perpendicular.

## 5. What artists do NOT mark

Paper-white reservation is its own technique, not a default. Sources:

- Bausenhardt (juliabausenhardt.com) — "leave white highlights on the paper from the start… focus on the main area of interest, or where there is a lot of contrast."
- Sketchbooks.org — explicit step "preserve blank space or sparse lines for highlights."
- Steinberg — large swaths of his page are literally untouched; tone is implied by adjacency.
- Niemann — minimalist editorial work uses paper-white as the dominant value.

Reservation rule: white is reserved for the brightest local plane and for high-contrast focal regions. A single sparse mark already shifts the region out of "paper-white" perceptually (Laws' threshold).

## 6. Synthesis — tonal-to-multi-axis mapping

Treating D as source darkness in [0, 1] and synthesizing across the canon. Recommendations are sourced ranges, not invented values.

| D | Register | Mark type | Gap (× strokeWidth) | Layer count | Weight (× base) | Ink opacity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00–0.10 | Paper-white reservation | none | — | 0 | — | — |
| 0.10–0.30 | Light grey | Single-direction hatch OR sparse stipple | 4–6× (gap-dominant; Lancaster/VI) | 1 | 0.5–0.7× (Smashing Pencils thin) | 0.6–0.8 (Laws' "lighter and closer") |
| 0.30–0.55 | Mid grey | Parallel hatch tightening toward contour hatch | 2–3× | 1 with directional rhythm shifts (Sketchbooks.org) | 0.7–1.0× | 0.8–1.0 |
| 0.55–0.80 | Dark grey | Cross-hatch (2 directions, offset 60–75° not 90°) | 1.5–2× | 2 | 1.0× | 1.0 |
| 0.80–1.00 | Near-black | Fine cross-hatch / Dürer double-hatch / tick accumulation | ≤1× (lines touching/overlapping) | 3–4 (HelloArtsy's 4-layer max) | 1.0–1.4× (Dürer's burin upshift) | 1.0 |

Worked example: for D = 0.20, the canon-blended recipe is **sparse single-direction hatch at gap = 4× strokeWidth, weight = 0.5–0.7× base, ink opacity 0.70**, with no second layer. This combines Lancaster (sparse), Smashing Pencils (thinner toward lighter), and Laws (lighter ink to suppress "line" reading).

Edge override: at any D, the mark closest to a lit boundary tapers to ~0.5× weight; the mark closest to a shadow boundary thickens to ~1.2–1.4× and may add a partial second-direction pass within ~1 gap-width of the boundary.

## 7. Honest gaps

- **No canonical numerical stair**. The "5-value scale" framing in lesson plans is a teaching convenience, not a master-tradition lock. Loomis, Dürer, Nice, Lancaster, Laws each bias differently. The renderer will have to **pick a bias** (gap-dominant vs layers-dominant vs weight-dominant) and own that choice.
- **No canonical opacity guidance**. Opacity is a digital surrogate for ink dilution / pressure. Laws is the closest source ("lighter and closer together") and it's perceptual, not numerical.
- **No canonical edge-falloff distance**. Sources say "thinner on lit side, thicker on shadow side" but nobody quantifies the falloff width. The renderer will have to invent (suggest: 1–2 gap-widths from the boundary).
- **No canonical mark-type switching threshold**. Sources agree techniques combine freely; nobody specifies "use hatch below D=X, switch to cross-hatch above." HelloArtsy's 4-layer scale is the only quasi-numerical anchor.
- **No canonical rule for mark direction across closed shapes**. Contour-hatch follows form; parallel-hatch is fixed. The choice is style, not value.
- **Perfect-freehand's pressure simulation is not in the canon at all.** It maps onto Smashing Pencils' pressure variation, but the parameter values (`thinning`, `streamline`) are pure engineering judgment.
